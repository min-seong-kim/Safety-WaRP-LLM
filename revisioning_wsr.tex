\begin{abstract}
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on downstream tasks often weakens their previously aligned safety behavior, creating an inherent trade-off  between task adaptation and safety preservation. Existing approaches attempt to mitigate this issue by identifying safety-relevant neurons, layers, or update directions in the original parameter space. However, safety-related information is often distributed across many directions in this space, causing downstream updates to overwrite parameters that encode safety behavior. 
We propose \textbf{WSR-Tune}, a \uline{W}eight \uline{S}pace \uline{R}eparameterization-based fine-tuning framework that preserves safety during downstream adaptation. Our approach constructs a safety-conditioned basis and reparameterizes the weight matrices such that safety-relevant information becomes concentrated in a small subset of directions. We then identify safety-critical directions and freeze them in the reparameterized space, while updating the remaining complementary directions for downstream adaptation. By explicitly structuring the parameter space to disentangle safety and task information, our method reduces interference between objectives and enables simultaneous preservation of safety and improvement in downstream performance. Experimental results show that our method achieves a more favorable trade-off between downstream performance and safety retention, demonstrating its effectiveness for reliable LLM fine-tuning. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}


Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in real-world settings where safety is critical~\citep{trustllm}. As their capabilities continue to advance, LLMs are assuming increasingly important roles across diverse domains such as healthcare, education, finance, and software engineering~\citep{bloomberggptl, chatgpt4good, gptcode, swebench}. Without adequate safety tuning, LLMs can provide harmful assistance, violate policies, and exhibit unsafe behavior at scale~\citep{chan0park}. Ensuring that these models remain helpful while consistently refusing harmful or disallowed requests has therefore become a central challenge in modern LLM development.

In practice, LLM deployment typically involves multiple post-training stages: a base model is first improved through instruction tuning and/or safety tuning, and is then further adapted to downstream tasks, domains, or user-specific objectives~\citep{ouyang2022training}. However, such downstream adaptation can degrade previously learned safety behaviors, making it difficult to maintain safety throughout the model life-cycle~\citep{huang2024lisa}. This issue becomes more critical as attempts to misuse LLMs have become increasingly sophisticated~\citep{yu2025infecting, li2025commercialllmagentsvulnerable, yang2024watch}. Recent work has introduced a wide range of jailbreak strategies, including manually crafted prompts~\citep{manual_prompt}, automated prompt optimization~\citep{adv-suffix, prompt-optimization}, adversarial fine-tuning~\citep{adv_finetuning, shadow_finetuning}, and other adaptive attacks~\citep{pair, pap, autodan, liar} designed to elicit harmful or policy-violating outputs even from safety-tuned models~\citep{jailbroken}. These threats suggest that safety is %not merely a narrow post-processing concern, but 
 an objective that must be preserved throughout model adaptation, deployment, and continual improvement.

A central challenge in LLM fine-tuning is thus preserving safety during downstream adaptation. To mitigate this issue, a growing body of work has attempted to explicitly preserve safety throughout downstream fine-tuning \citep{safeInstr,sn-tune,safe-lora,safe-delta,Resta}. The authors of %\textit{SafeInstr} 
\citep{safeInstr} show that adding a small amount of safety data during fine-tuning can provide  safety gains, although excessive safety tuning may induce over-refusal. In \citep{sn-tune}, the authors propose SN-Tune and RSN-Tune, which identify safety-specific neurons and selectively update them to improve safety during downstream adaptation. Other approaches instead modify post-fine-tuning updates: %Safe LoRA \citep{safe-lora} constrains low-rank updates through alignment-aware projection, 
 Safe Delta \citep{safe-delta} adjusts delta parameters based on their estimated safety degradation and utility contribution, and applies a safety compensation vector.  Resta \citep{Resta} restores safety by adding a safety vector to the compromised model weights. These methods show that safety can be partially preserved or recovered during downstream adaptation. 



 


\textbf{Challenges.} However, despite prior efforts, existing methods share a key limitation: they operate entirely in the \textit{original parameterization} of the model. For example, consider a safety-tuned $2 \times 2$ weight matrix $W$ from an arbitrary layer, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:11}
W = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix}= w_{11} b_{11}  + w_{12} b_{12} +  w_{21} b_{21} + w_{22} b_{22},
\end{equation}
where $\{w_{ij}\}$ are the model parameters, $\{b_{ij}\}$ are the canonical basis matrices, and each $b_{ij}$ has a single nonzero entry of 1 at position $(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere (e.g., {\scriptsize $b_{11} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$}, {\scriptsize $b_{22} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$}). 


In this representation, safety-related information is not localized to a specific coordinate $w_{ij}$, but is instead distributed across multiple directions in the parameter space (i.e., across  $w_{11}$, $w_{12}$, $w_{21}$, $w_{22}$). Consequently, even if prior methods identify and preserve safety-relevant parameters, downstream adaptation can still overwrite other parameters that encode safety behavior.  In other words, preserving a subset of parameters in the original space is often insufficient, as the parameterization does not provide a clear separation between safety-preservation and task adaptation.

This limitation is empirically demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:basis_rotation_motivation}.  When a safety-tuned model is fine-tuned on a downstream task, full parameter adaptation severely degrades safety. Freezing a subset of parameters (e.g., 10\%, 30\%, 50\%) in the original space during downstream adaptation only partially alleviates this issue. In contrast, applying the same freeze-and-adapt procedure after reparameterization preserves safety much more effectively, while maintaining sufficient capacity for downstream adaptation.

 These results suggest that robust safety preservation cannot be achieved solely by selecting parameters to freeze in the original space; rather, it requires reparameterizing the model so that safety-preserving and task-adaptive directions are more easily disentangled.  
We thus pose the following key question: 

\textit{Can we construct a weight-space representation that concentrates safety-relevant information into a compact subspace, while leaving the remaining directions available for downstream adaptation?}



\textbf{Contributions.} Motivated by this question, we propose WSR-Tune, a safety-aware LLM fine-tuning approach based on \textit{weight-space reparameterization}.  Specifically, we replace the above canonical parameterization in \eqref{eq:11} with a reparameterized form as

\begin{equation}
W = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{w}_{11} \phi_{11}  + \tilde{w}_{12} \phi_{12} +  \tilde{w}_{21} \phi_{21} + \tilde{w}_{22} \phi_{22},
\end{equation}
where ${\phi_{ij}}$ indicates a new basis and ${\tilde{w}_{ij}}$ are the corresponding weights in the reparameterized space. Unlike the canonical basis where each $b_{ij}$ contains a single nonzero entry, the new basis $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ can consist of directions with multiple nonzero entries.



Building on this framework, we construct a safety-conditioned basis $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ and reparameterize the weight space from $\{{w}_{ij}\}$ to $\{{\tilde{w}_{ij}}\}$  such that safety-relevant behavior is concentrated in a small subset of directions (i.e., only a few basis directions in $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ capture safety-relevant information), rather than being diffusely distributed. This leaves the complementary directions with sufficient capacity for downstream adaptation, as illustrated in  Figure~\ref{fig:WSR-Tune_figure}. Leveraging the low-rank structure of activations, safety-critical weights can be more clearly identified and isolated in the reparameterized space. We then freeze these weight parameters and update only the complementary ones for downstream adaptation. As a result, our approach reduces interference between previously learned safety behavior and downstream adaptation, enabling more effective safety-preserving fine-tuning.


Our WSR-Tune can be seamlessly integrated with existing downstream adaptation methods originally designed for the original parameter space, by applying them in the reparameterized space without modifying their update rules.
 Experimental results demonstrate that our method better preserves safety during downstream fine-tuning and achieves a more favorable trade-off between downstream performance and safety retention, compared to methods operating in the original parameter space.

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}
\vspace{-1.5mm}


\textbf{Preserving safety during downstream adaptation.} 
Recent work on LLM safety has focused on preserving safety during post-training and downstream fine-tuning~\citep{adv_finetuning, antidote}. SafeInstr~\citep{safeInstr} shows that utilizing a small amount of safety data during fine-tuning can improve safety, although excessive safety tuning may induce exaggerated safety behaviors. Lisa~\citep{lisa} alternates optimization over safety-alignment and downstream data while constraining optimization drift with a proximal term. SN-Tune and RSN-Tune~\citep{sn-tune} take a mechanistic approach by identifying safety-specific neurons and selectively tuning or separating them to improve robustness during downstream adaptation. AsFT~\citep{asft} constrains parameter updates within a narrow safety basin by penalizing directions orthogonal to the safety-alignment direction, while SafeGrad~\citep{safegrad} resolves conflicts between downstream and safety gradients through gradient surgery. SEAL~\citep{seal} instead adopts a data-centric approach, using bilevel optimization to select safety-compatible fine-tuning samples. For parameter-efficient adaptation, SaLoRA~\citep{salora} introduces a fixed safety module to constrain low-rank updates from disrupting safety-related features. Other methods instead modify post-fine-tuning updates: Safe LoRA~\citep{safe-lora} constrains low-rank updates with an alignment-aware projection, Safe Delta~\citep{safe-delta} adjusts delta parameters in a safety-aware manner while compensating residual safety loss, and RESTA~\citep{Resta} restores safety by adding a safety vector to the fine-tuned model weights. These studies show that safety is highly fragile under downstream adaptation and requires explicit mechanisms for preservation. However, most of these methods still attempt to protect safety within the original parameterization of the model. In contrast, WSR-Tune is a fine-tuning-stage defense that reparameterizes the weight space before downstream adaptation and preserves safety-critical coefficients throughout fine-tuning, enabling a more structured separation between safety preservation and task adaptation.

\textbf{Analyzing safety in representation and parameter space.} 
Other recent works have analyzed LLM safety through more structured dimensions and spaces~\citep{zou2025representationengineeringtopdownapproach, geometry_cone}. A recent study \citep{hidden_dimensions} shows that safety-aligned behavior is not governed by a single refusal direction, but by a multi-dimensional residual space in which a dominant direction captures refusal while additional orthogonal directions encode other safety-related features. A complementary line of work \citep{assessing_the_brittleness} further identify safety-critical components at the rank level using ActSVD. ActSVD preserves coupled output-rank components, whereas WSR-Tune constructs a complete input space basis from safety activations and selects coefficients within this reparameterized space, allowing different output channels to preserve different subsets of safety directions. We provide a detailed comparison and fine-tuning-stage ActSVD baselines in Appendix~\ref{app:actsved}. \kms{Reviewer 2의 ActSVD와의 차이점 언급. 자세한 내용과 ActSVD의 rank/neuron 비교 실험은 appendix에 추가} Safety appears to be highly entangled with general learning rather than isolated in a distinct linear subspace, as both weight-space and activation-space analyses show substantial overlap between safety-related and utility-related directions \cite{Safety_subspace_arenot_linearly_distinct}. Overall, these studies suggest that safety is distributed across directions, ranks, and subspaces rather than being cleanly localized to a small set of canonical parameters~\citep{li2025safety, geometry_cone}. This in turn implies that post-hoc selection of important components in the original space may be insufficient for robust safety preservation.

\textbf{Subspace-constrained adaptation.} Although not focused on safety, a line of prior work studies how to reduce interference by constraining parameter updates within structured subspaces. AlphaEdit \citep{alphaedit} projects updates into the null space of preserved knowledge, while orthogonal fine-tuning methods such as PSOFT \citep{psoft} restrict adaptation to directions that are orthogonal to important components. These methods mitigate interference by carefully selecting update directions, but they still operate within the original parameterization of the model.  In a related but distinct direction, \citep{warp} explores weight space rotation primarily in the context of class-incremental learning. While this highlights the potential of restructuring the parameter space, it is not designed to address safety preservation during downstream adaptation. These works motivate our view that the safety problem is not only about identifying which parameters matter, but also about choosing a more suitable space in which preservation and adaptation can be disentangled. \kms{오히려 subspace adaptation이 꼭 필요할지..}

\section{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

\vspace{-1mm}

\subsection{Problem setup}

\vspace{-1mm}

We consider adapting an LLM to downstream tasks while preserving its previously learned safety behaviors. Let $f_{W_0}$ denote a safety-tuned model with parameters $W_0$, where “safety-tuned” indicates that the model has acquired the ability to refuse harmful or disallowed requests through prior safety post-training. In many practical settings, such a model is subsequently adapted to a downstream task using a task-specific dataset $D_{\mathrm{task}}$. However, standard fine-tuning on $D_{\mathrm{task}}$ often degrades the model’s previously learned safety behaviors, even when the downstream data itself is benign. This leads to an inherent trade-off between improving downstream task performance and preserving safety during adaptation.


Formally, let $\mathcal{L}(W; D)$ denote the loss of model $W$ on dataset $D$, and let $D_{\mathrm{safe}}$ denote a safety-oriented dataset used to preserve safe behavior. In conventional fine-tuning, adaptation is performed by applying a parameter update $\Delta W$ to $W_0$ in the original weight space. Our objective is to improve downstream performance while preserving safety: 
\begin{equation} \min_{\Delta W}\ \mathcal{L}(W_0 + \Delta W; D_{\mathrm{task}}) \ \ \text{subject to} \ \  \mathcal{L}(W_0 + \Delta W; D_{\mathrm{safe}}) \leq \mathcal{L}(W_0; D_{\mathrm{safe}}) + \epsilon, 
\end{equation} 
where $\epsilon \geq 0$ denotes a tolerable increase in safety loss. 




\subsection{Motivation and Insight}

Despite extensive efforts, existing approaches \citep{safeInstr,sn-tune,safe-lora,safe-delta,Resta} operate in the original parameter space. To understand the limitation of this representation, consider 
an arbitrary  $m\times n$ weight matrix
\begin{equation}
W =
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{11} & \cdots & w_{1n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
w_{m1} & \cdots & w_{mn}
\end{bmatrix}= %\sum_{ij}w_{ij}b_{ij}
\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^nw_{ij}b_{ij}
\end{equation}
of a \textit{safety-tuned} model, where $\{w_{ij}\}$ are the model parameters and $\{b_{ij}\}$ are the canonical basis matrices. Each $b_{ij}$ has a single nonzero entry of 1 at position $(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere.

In this coordinate system, safety-related information is not localized to a specific coordinate $w_{ij}$, but is instead distributed across multiple directions in the parameter space. Consequently, downstream fine-tuning (even when applied to only a subset of parameters) can still overwrite safety-related information encoded in other parameters, leading to degraded safety. This hypothesis is supported by our preliminary results in Table~\ref{tab:basis_rotation_motivation}, where  freezing a certain fraction of parameters in the original space is insufficient to adequately preserve safety. This raises the following question:

\textit{Is the original coordinate system the most suitable for fine-tuning, or can we construct a better basis that enables effective downstream  adaptation without compromising safety?}







\subsection{WSR-Tune: Reparameterized Fine-Tuning for Safety Preservation}
\label{sec:wsr-algorithm}
We propose WSR-Tune, which moves beyond identifying \textit{what} to protect and instead reconsider \textit{how} the parameter space itself is represented.  To this end, we transform the weight space into a safety-conditioned coordinate system in which safety-relevant behavior is more compactly concentrated, making it easier to preserve during downstream fine-tuning. We replace the canonical parameterization 

\begin{equation}
W = \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^nw_{ij}b_{ij}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{Reparameterization}}\;
W = \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n \tilde{w}_{ij} \phi_{ij}, 
\end{equation}

where $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ denotes a new basis and $\{\tilde{w}_{ij}\}$ are the corresponding weights in the reparameterized space. Unlike the canonical basis, where each $b_{ij}$ contains a single nonzero entry, the new basis matrices can represent structured directions spanning multiple parameters. The reparameterized weight matrix $\tilde{W}$ can be expressed in terms of the new basis $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ as
\begin{equation}
\tilde{W} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\tilde{w}_{11} & \cdots & \tilde{w}_{1n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\tilde{w}_{m1} & \cdots & \tilde{w}_{mn}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\langle W, \phi_{11} \rangle & \cdots & \langle W, \phi_{1n} \rangle \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\langle W, \phi_{m1} \rangle & \cdots & \langle W, \phi_{mn} \rangle
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ denotes the Frobenius inner product between two matrices. 


This transformation aims to make safety-critical directions more identifiable and concentrated in the reparameterized space, while leaving the complementary directions with sufficient capacity for adaptation. Our key idea is to perform adaptation by updating a subset of $\tilde{w}_{ij}$, rather than directly modifying the original parameters $w_{ij}$, thereby better preserving safety during downstream adaptation. The high-level idea of WSR-Tune is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:WSR-Tune_figure}. In the following, we describe how our reparameterization is constructed and applied in practice.


\textbf{Safety-aware weight reparameterization.} Our first step is to construct a new basis $\{\phi_{ij}\}$ in which safety-relevant information is concentrated in a small subset of directions. Let $W_l \in \mathbb{R}^{m_l \times n_l}$ denote the weight matrix of the $l$-th selected layer. For each sample $x_i$ and token position $t \in [T_i]$, let $h_l(x_i,t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ denote the input activation of the $t$-th token to layer $W_l$, i.e., the hidden representation of that token produced by the preceding layers before being transformed by $W_l$. We then collect these token-wise activations to form
\begin{equation}
H_l = [\, h_l(x_i,t) \,]_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{safe}}}
\in \mathbb{R}^{n_l \times M},
\end{equation}
where $T_i$ denotes the number of valid tokens in sample $x_i$, and $M = \sum_{i=1}^N T_i$ is the total number of token-wise activations collected from samples $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from a safety dataset $D_{\mathrm{safe}}$. We then compute the activation covariance and perform singular value decomposition:
\begin{equation}
H_l H_l^\top = U_l \Sigma_l U_l^\top,
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.42\textwidth}
    \vspace{-6mm}
    \centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/layer_15_svd_dist.png}

    }
    \label{fig:layer_15}
    \vspace{-1mm}
\end{wrapfigure}
where $U_l \in \mathbb{R}^{n_l \times n_l}$ is an orthonormal matrix whose columns are ordered according to the singular values in $\Sigma_l$. Figure~\ref{fig:layer_15} shows that the singular values exhibit a clear low-rank structure, motivating the use of $U_l$ as an activation-induced basis.


We thus reparameterize the weight matrix as 
\begin{equation} 
\widetilde{W}_l  = V_l^\top W_l U_l, 
\end{equation} 
where $U_l$ is defined above, $V_l$ is an arbitrary unitary matrix, and $\widetilde{W}_l \in \mathbb{R}^{m_l \times n_l}$ denotes the weights in the reparameterized space. Since $U_l$ is an orthonormal basis of the input activation space of $W_l$, Eq.~(9) corresponds to a change of input coordinates: with $\widetilde h_l=U_l^\top h_l$, we have $W_lh_l=V_l\widetilde W_l\widetilde h_l$. \kms{Reviewer 2의 $U_l$이 Eq.(9)에서 어떻게 applicable한지 설명, 굳이 이렇게 같은 좌표계로 맞게 변환된다고 말을 해야할지..} Importantly, this SVD is performed only once before downstream fine-tuning to construct $U_l$, which is then kept fixed throughout adaptation. \kms{SVD 계산은 Fine-tuning 전 한번만 수행한다고 언급.} Let $\{v_i\}_{i=1}^{m_l}$ and $\{u_j\}_{j=1}^{n_l}$ denote the column vectors of $V_l$ and $U_l$, respectively.  
Introducing $V_l$ allows us to define a complete orthonormal basis over the matrix space via outer products of row and column directions, i.e., 
\begin{equation}
\phi_{ij} = v_i u_j^\top, 
\end{equation}
ensuring that each $(i,j)$ corresponds to a distinct basis element. In this work, we set $V_l=I$ for simplicity, avoiding arbitrary data-independent rotations of the output coordinates; Appendix~\ref{app:v_basis} shows that WSR-Tune remains robust to alternative orthogonal choices of $V_l$. \kms{Reviewer 1의 $V_l$ matrix의 basis choice 실험} Under this formulation, each entry $\widetilde{W}_l(i,j)=\tilde{w}_{ij}$ represents the reparameterized weight along the basis element $\phi_{ij}$. This reparameterization defines a coordinate system aligned with activation statistics, in which safety-related information can be represented more compactly.



\textbf{Identifying safety-critical parameters.} Under this reparameterization, downstream adaptation is applied to the coefficients of $\widetilde{W}_l$, rather than to $W_l$. 
To preserve safety, within each layer, we estimate the importance of each coefficient using its gradient magnitude on a safety corpus:
\begin{equation}
G_l = \sum_{x \in D_{\mathrm{safe}}}
\left|
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(x)}{\partial \widetilde{W}_l}
\right|.
\end{equation}
Here, $G_l(i,j)$ quantifies the sensitivity of the safety loss to $\widetilde{W}_l(i,j)$, and larger values indicate greater importance for safety retention. Based on $G_l$, we construct a binary mask $M_l \in \{0,1\}^{m_l \times n_l}$, where $M_l(i,j)=1$ denotes a safety-critical coefficients to be preserved in the reparameterized space. \kms{Reviewer 1이 헷갈려 한 masking이 적용되는 sapce는 reparameterzed space 언급.} In practice, we select the top-$\rho$ fraction of entries according to $G_l$ within each layer, where $\rho \in [0,1]$ controls the trade-off between safety retention and downstream adaptability. 


\textbf{Downstream adaptation in the complementary subspace.} During downstream fine-tuning, we update only the complementary coefficients while blocking gradients through the masked ones:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{W}_l \leftarrow
\mathrm{stopgrad}(\widetilde{W}_l \odot M_l)
+
\widetilde{W}_l \odot (1 - M_l),
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. This constrains adaptation to the complementary subspace, while preserving the coefficients most critical for safety in the reparameterized space. 

Importantly, WSR-Tune does not rely on a new importance-scoring or masking primitive; rather, its key distinction is the safety-conditioned coordinate system in which these operations are applied. With the default $V_l=I$, freezing a coefficient $\widetilde{W}_l(i,j)$ imposes
\begin{equation}
    e_i^\top \Delta W_l u_j = 0,
\end{equation}
so an element-wise mask in the reparameterized space induces a structured linear constraint over the original weights. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial \widetilde{W}_l}
    =
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_l}U_l,
\end{equation}
showing that the same gradient-based importance rule yields a different ranking after reparameterization. Thus, WSR-Tune changes both which coefficients are identified as safety-critical and the admissible update geometry induced by protecting them.
% Our WSR-Tune differs from standard fine-tuning in two key aspects. First, it decouples basis construction from task adaptation: a safety-conditioned basis is constructed once using safety data and kept fixed thereafter. Second, protection is applied in the reparameterized space, where coefficients are aligned with safety-relevant activation directions. This enables more targeted preservation of safety and reduces interference during downstream fine-tuning.

\subsection{Extension to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning: WSR-LoRA}
\label{sec:wsr_lora}

WSR-Tune can also be extended to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT).
Under standard LoRA, a safety-aligned weight $W_0$ is kept frozen while a
low-rank update is learned as
\begin{equation}
    W_t = W_0 + B_t A_t.
\end{equation}
However, since the LoRA update is initialized to zero, the safety-related
structure of the initial model is contained in the frozen $W_0$ rather than
in the trainable adapter. Consequently, directly masking entries of the
standard LoRA factors does not explicitly preserve the safety-aligned
coefficients encoded in $W_0$.

To address this, we initialize the low-rank adapter from a rank-$r$
decomposition of $W_0$ and absorb the remaining component into a frozen
residual,
\begin{equation}
    W_0 = W_{\mathrm{res}} + B_0 A_0.
\end{equation}
We then apply the safety-conditioned reparameterization to the input-side
factor, defining $\widetilde{A}_t=A_tU$ and parameterizing
\begin{equation}
    W_t =
    W_{\mathrm{res}} +
    B_t \widetilde{A}_t U^\top.
\end{equation}
This construction exactly recovers $W_0$ at initialization while placing
the trainable low-rank component in the same safety-conditioned input
coordinates used by WSR-Tune. We then identify and preserve safety-critical
entries of $B_t$ and $\widetilde{A}_t$ using the same importance-based
masking principle as in Eqs.~(11)--(12), while keeping
$W_{\mathrm{res}}$ and $U$ fixed. We refer to this PEFT extension as
\textbf{WSR-LoRA}. Detailed initialization and training procedures are
provided in Appendix~\ref{app:wsr_lora}.








\subsection{Discussion}

We discuss several practical aspects of WSR-Tune.

\textbf{Applicability to different layers.} WSR-Tune can be applied not only to MLP layers but also to attention layers, including the key, query, and value projection matrices. In our experiments in Section \ref{sec:experiments}, we apply WSR-Tune to both MLP and transformer layers and evaluate its effectiveness.

\textbf{Compatibility with prior works.} WSR-Tune can be integrated with existing  methods such as SafeInstr and SN-Tune by applying them in the reparameterized space. We validate this in Section \ref{sec:experiments}.


\textbf{Freeze ratio.} The freeze ratio $\rho$ controls the fraction of coefficients preserved for safety. In Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, we study the impact of varying $\rho$, demonstrating its role in balancing safety and downstream performance. Notably, WSR-Tune achieves a strong trade-off even with relatively small values of $\rho$.

\subsection{Experimental Setup \& Details}
\vspace{-1mm}



\textbf{Datasets and models.} We use the Circuit Breakers dataset~\citep{circuit_breakers} as the safety dataset, consisting of harmful prompts paired with safe refusal responses. For downstream adaptation, we use GSM8K~\citep{gsm8k}, Hendrycks MATH~\citep{hendrycksmath}, MedQA~\citep{medqa}, and ARC-Challenge~\citep{arc-c}. We conduct experiments on a diverse set of large language models spanning the Llama\citep{llama2, llama3}, Qwen~\citep{qwen2.5}, and Gemma~\citep{gemma2} families, and report the exact model configurations in the corresponding result tables.




\textbf{Baselines.}

 We compare WSR-Tune with representative safety-preserving fine-tuning baselines: full-parameter fine-tuning, SafeInstr~\citep{safeInstr}, Resta~\citep{Resta}, SafeDelta~\citep{safe-delta}, SN-Tune, and RSN-Tune~\citep{sn-tune}. Full-parameter fine-tuning updates all model weights without safety constraints. SafeInstr is a data-based baseline, for which we add safety examples corresponding to 10\% of the downstream training set. Resta and SafeDelta are post-hoc weight-space baselines: Resta adds a safety vector to the fine-tuned model with scaling coefficient 0.3, while SafeDelta adjusts fine-tuning deltas using a safety degradation constraint with $s=0.1$. SN-Tune and RSN-Tune restrict updates to safety-relevant regions, limiting safety neurons or critical safety neurons  to within 1\% of the model parameters.  

 \textbf{Evaluation metrics.}
We evaluate downstream performance using 5-shot accuracy on each downstream dataset. For safety evaluation, we use harmful prompts from AdvBench~\citep{advbench} and report attack success rate (ASR). We consider one direct prompting setting using the original AdvBench prompts, and three jailbreak attack methods, AutoDAN~\citep{autodan}, PAIR~\citep{pair}, and PAP~\citep{pap}, which generate attack prompts. We generate jailbreak prompts using the HarmBench~\citep{harmbench} and measure ASR with its refusal keywords, counting an attack as successful if the response does not begin with these keywords.

\textbf{Implementation details.}
Unless otherwise specified, all methods follow the same downstream fine-tuning pipeline and are trained for 3 epochs, with method-specific learning rates chosen to balance downstream performance and safety retention; full hyperparameters are provided in the Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameters}. SafeInstr updates all model weights using the augmented training set, whereas Resta and SafeDelta are applied post hoc to the downstream-tuned checkpoint. For SN-Tune, RSN-Tune, and WSR-Tune, we target all transformer layers and apply the method to the attention projections \texttt{q}, \texttt{k}, and \texttt{v} and the MLP projections \texttt{up} and \texttt{down}. WSR-Tune constructs the safety-conditioned basis from the Circuit Breakers dataset and uses a freeze ratio of 10\% in the reparameterized space. The effect of varying the freeze ratio is further analyzed.

\section{Conclusion and Limitation} 
\vspace{-1mm}

In this work, we revisited safety-preserving fine-tuning in LLMs and showed that existing approaches are fundamentally constrained by operating in the original parameter space, where safety information is distributed across directions. We proposed WSR-Tune, a weight-space reparameterization framework that constructs a safety-conditioned basis, enabling downstream adaptation in a complementary subspace. By concentrating safety-relevant information into a small set of coefficients, WSR-Tune allows effective preservation of safety through simple freezing while maintaining strong downstream performance. %Extensive experiments across models and tasks demonstrate that WSR-Tune consistently achieves a more favorable safety-utility trade-off compared to prior methods. 
Our results suggest that safety preservation depends not only on which parameters are updated, but also on how the parameter space is structured, highlighting weight-space reparameterization as a promising direction for reliable LLM adaptation. A limitation of our work is the additional computational overhead introduced by the SVD computation and the estimation of safety-related coefficients; we report the per-epoch training time in the Appendix~\ref{app:runtime}. Future work may further combine this perspective with low-rank adaptation, enabling parameter-efficient updates that remain explicitly separated from safety-sensitive directions.

\section{Comparison with ActSVD}
\label{app:actsved}
\kms{Rebuttal에서 설명한 내용 그대로 설명 및 실험 서술}
We further clarify the distinction between WSR-Tune and ActSVD~\citep{assessing_the_brittleness}, as both methods use activation-derived structure but differ in the space and granularity at which preservation is performed.

\paragraph{ActSVD.}
Let $W_l\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ denote a linear projection and $X_l\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times N}$ its input activations. ActSVD performs SVD on the projected activations,
\begin{equation}
    W_lX_l \in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times N},
\end{equation}
and uses the leading output singular vectors
$U_{l,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$
to construct
\begin{equation}
    W_l^{c}
    =
    U_{r}
    (U_{r})^\top W_l.
\end{equation}
It further isolates safety-specific modifications through projections such as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta W_l
    =
    (I-\Pi_l^{u})\Pi_l^{s}W_l.
\end{equation}
Thus, ActSVD operates on coupled output-rank components: once a singular direction is selected, the corresponding rank component is jointly preserved or removed.

\paragraph{WSR-Tune.}
WSR-Tune instead constructs a complete basis from the covariance of safety input activations,
\begin{equation}
    H_lH_l^\top
    =
    U_l\Sigma_lU_l^\top,
\end{equation}
and reparameterizes the weight matrix as
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde W_l
    =
    V_l^\top W_lU_l.
\end{equation}
Safety importance is then evaluated for each coefficient
$\widetilde W_l(i,j)$, and downstream adaptation is restricted by
\begin{equation}
    \Delta W_l
    =
    V_l
    \left(
        \Delta\widetilde W_l
        \odot (1-M_l)
    \right)
    U_l^\top.
\end{equation}
Therefore, WSR-Tune does not preserve an entire rank component. Its preservation unit is an individual coefficient in the reparameterized basis, and the resulting update is not required to be low-rank.

With the default $V_l=I$, let
\begin{equation}
    S_{l,i}=\{j:M_l(i,j)=1\}
\end{equation}
denote the basis directions protected for output row $i$. The corresponding projector is
\begin{equation}
    P_{l,i}
    =
    \sum_{j\in S_{l,i}}u_ju_j^\top.
\end{equation}
Hence, different output rows can preserve different subsets of the safety-conditioned input basis. In contrast, ActSVD couples parameters through shared output-rank components. This provides WSR-Tune with a finer-grained preservation mechanism than rank-level projection.

The distinction also applies to importance scoring. Since
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial \widetilde W_l}
    =
    V_l^\top
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_l}
    U_l,
\end{equation}
coordinate-wise importance ranking depends on the chosen basis. Thus, applying the same gradient-based criterion after reparameterization generally identifies a different protected subspace from applying it directly in the original parameterization.

To directly compare the two approaches, we adapt ActSVD into downstream fine-tuning-stage defenses at both the rank and neuron levels. \textsc{ActSVD-Tuned (rank)} preserves the safety-relevant output-rank components identified from

\section{Detailed Formulation of WSR-LoRA}
\label{app:wsr_lora}

We provide the detailed initialization and optimization procedure for
WSR-LoRA. For clarity, we omit the layer index below; the procedure is
applied independently to each adapted linear projection.

\paragraph{Safety-preserving low-rank initialization.}
Let $W_0\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ denote a safety-aligned weight matrix.
We first compute its singular value decomposition
\begin{equation}
    W_0 = P\Lambda Q^\top.
\end{equation}
Using the leading $r$ singular components, we initialize
\begin{equation}
    B_0 = P_r\Lambda_r^{1/2},
    \qquad
    A_0 = \Lambda_r^{1/2}Q_r^\top,
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{equation}
    B_0A_0 = P_r\Lambda_rQ_r^\top.
\end{equation}
The remaining component is absorbed into a frozen residual weight,
\begin{equation}
    W_{\mathrm{res}}
    =
    W_0-B_0A_0.
\end{equation}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    W_0
    =
    W_{\mathrm{res}}+B_0A_0,
\end{equation}
so the low-rank factors are initialized from an actual component of the
safety-aligned weight rather than from a zero update.

\paragraph{Safety-conditioned reparameterization.}
Let $U$ denote the safety-conditioned input basis constructed as in
Section~\ref{sec:wsr-algorithm}. We reparameterize the input-side factor as
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde A = AU,
    \qquad
    A=\widetilde A U^\top,
\end{equation}
and write the adapted weight as
\begin{equation}
    W_t
    =
    W_{\mathrm{res}}
    +
    B_t\widetilde A_tU^\top.
\end{equation}
At initialization, we set
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde A_0=A_0U.
\end{equation}
Since $UU^\top=I$,
\begin{align}
    W_{\mathrm{res}}
    +B_0\widetilde A_0U^\top
    =
    W_{\mathrm{res}}
    +B_0A_0UU^\top
    =
    W_{\mathrm{res}}+B_0A_0
    =
    W_0,
\end{align}
showing that WSR-LoRA exactly preserves the original safety-aligned model
at initialization.

\paragraph{Importance estimation and masking.}
During downstream adaptation, $W_{\mathrm{res}}$ and $U$ remain fixed,
while $B$ and $\widetilde A$ are trainable. Following Eq.~(11), we compute
element-wise safety importance for both factors:
\begin{equation}
    G_B
    =
    \sum_{x\in D_{\mathrm{safe}}}
    \left|
    \frac{\partial L(x)}{\partial B}
    \right|,
    \qquad
    G_{\widetilde A}
    =
    \sum_{x\in D_{\mathrm{safe}}}
    \left|
    \frac{\partial L(x)}{\partial \widetilde A}
    \right|.
\end{equation}
We then construct binary masks $M_B$ and $M_{\widetilde A}$ using the same
top-$\rho$ importance rule as WSR-Tune. During downstream fine-tuning, the
selected safety-critical entries of $B$ and $\widetilde A$ are kept fixed at
their initialized values, while only the remaining entries are updated.

Unlike full-parameter WSR-Tune, where freezing a reparameterized coefficient
directly imposes a linear constraint on $\Delta W$, the effective WSR-LoRA
update depends bilinearly on $B$ and $\widetilde A$. Thus, masking the two
factors constrains the corresponding safety-conditioned responses through
their low-rank factorization rather than imposing the exact coefficient-wise
constraint of full-parameter WSR-Tune.

\subsection{Evaluation with Model-Based Safety Classifiers}
\label{app:model_based_evaluation}



The main results use refusal-keyword matching to measure attack success rate (ASR). To examine whether the conclusions depend on this heuristic, we additionally score the same generated responses with Beaver-Dam-7B~\citep{beavertails}, a moderation model trained to classify harmful assistant responses. Whereas keyword matching inspects only the prefix of a response, Beaver-Dam-7B judges the full response content, so a non-refusal prefix is not counted as a successful attack when the completion itself is harmless. Absolute ASR values under the two protocols are therefore not directly comparable, and we report Beaver-Dam ASR averaged over Direct, AutoDAN, PAIR, and PAP. Under this evaluator the relative ordering of the methods is preserved: SN-Tune and RSN-Tune remain markedly less safe than full-parameter fine-tuning on the larger models, while WSR-Tune maintains low classifier-based ASR together with competitive downstream performance. Using the same overall score as in Table~\ref{tab:main_all_models_delta}, WSR-Tune ranks first on three of the four models and second on the remaining one, where it is within $0.01$ points of the best baseline.
